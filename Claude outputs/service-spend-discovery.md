# Existing Human Service Spend — Where Software Hasn't Replaced People
**16 September 2026 · Evidence only · No ranking · No recommendation**

Method: searched for published service prices first, then asked what the human does and whether software has eliminated it. Every price below is from a vendor's own published pricing or a third-party comparison I opened.

**I found four categories that pass all five of your final filters, not five to ten.** Two more are listed with their evidence gaps. I'm not padding to hit a number.

**One structural finding you should read before the categories:** the two clearest cases of "software exists and humans are still paid" are both **contingency-priced** — the vendor takes a percentage of money recovered. That pricing model exists precisely because the buyer won't pay upfront for an uncertain outcome. Converting either to a subscription means fighting the pricing model the market has already chosen. That's in §A and §B and it matters more than any individual finding.

# PASSES ALL FIVE FILTERS

## A. Chargeback representment

**What is sold:** fighting credit-card disputes on behalf of e-commerce merchants — assembling the evidence package, submitting it to the processor, tracking outcomes.

**Who pays:** e-commerce merchant owner or operations lead. Non-technical.

**How much** ([Redo's line-by-line pricing comparison, July 2026](https://redo.com/resources/articles/comparisons/chargeback-representment-pricing)):

| Vendor | Representment | Deflection | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chargeflow | **25% of recovered** | $29 per deflection | $0.20/transaction |
| Disputifier | **20% of recovered, capped $250/case** | Custom | $0.05/order |
| Redo Reclaim | $0 | Not published | Not published |
| Chargebacks911 | Not published | Not published | Not published |

Plus processor dispute fees of **$15–$25 and up per dispute**, separate and often non-refundable even on a win.

**Why the customer can't DIY:** fragmented systems (evidence lives in the store platform, the shipping carrier, the payment processor and the support inbox), tight deadlines, processor-specific evidence formats, and reason-code-specific argumentation. Structural, not "they're busy."

**What the human actually does — and this is the key evidence:** the comparison states plainly that *"someone still supplies policy documents, tracking, and fulfilment data, and someone still reads the outcomes."* Automation handles evidence assembly, but each case still requires **"20 to 45 minutes of somebody's attention"** for preparation and final review.

**Software that exists:** Chargeflow, Disputifier, Chargeblast, Chargebacks911, Redo — all software-led, all still charging for outcomes rather than access.

**What remains manual despite the software:** evidence sourcing from systems the tool doesn't reach; judgement on which cases are worth fighting; reading and acting on outcomes.

**Recurrence:** continuous. Disputes arrive every week a merchant trades.

**Liability:** low. A lost dispute costs the merchant money already at risk. No regulated activity, no licence.

**Acquisition:** Shopify App Store and other commerce marketplaces, comparison content, SEO. Product-led is normal here. No cold calling required.

**Competitive gap evidence:** merchants pay 20–25% of recovered funds *to companies that already have the software*. If the software fully solved it, the fee would be a subscription.

**The trap:** contingency pricing is the market norm. A fixed-fee product competes against "you only pay when we win," which is a very strong offer to a cash-conscious merchant.

## B. Amazon FBA reimbursement recovery

**What is sold:** finding and claiming money Amazon owes a seller — lost or damaged inventory, incorrect fees, mis-shipments — going back 18 months.

**Who pays:** Amazon FBA seller or brand operator. Non-technical.

**How much** ([Getida pricing](https://revenuegeeks.com/software/getida/pricing)): **25% commission per successful recovery.** No subscription, setup or licensing fee. First $400 of recoveries free. Invoiced monthly after recovery.

**Why the customer can't DIY:** reconciling Amazon's settlement and inventory reports against what physically happened requires data work most sellers can't do, and each claim needs documentation assembled in Amazon's expected format within eligibility windows.

**What the human actually does:** the automated part identifies discrepancies. The human part — explicitly described — is that a *"professional team reconciles figures and gathers required documentation,"* opens and manages reimbursement cases directly with Amazon, and handles ongoing communication through the process.

**Software that exists:** Getida, Refund Pros, Aura, Signalytics, Amplisell and others. Multiple comparison roundups exist, which indicates a real buying market.

**What remains manual:** case correspondence with Amazon, documentation assembly, and the judgement about which discrepancies are worth claiming.

**Recurrence:** continuous, tied to order volume.

**Liability:** low.

**Acquisition:** Amazon seller communities, comparison content, seller conferences, app directories. Async is normal.

**The trap:** same contingency problem as chargebacks, plus platform dependency — Amazon can change its reimbursement policy or automate it away unilaterally, and has periodically tightened claim rules.

## C. Multi-state entity compliance filing

**What is sold:** tracking and filing annual reports for business entities registered in multiple states, keeping them in good standing.

**Who pays:** business owner, compliance professional, corporate paralegal, or an accounting firm filing on clients' behalf. Non-technical.

**How much** ([Harbor Compliance annual report service](https://www.harborcompliance.com/annual-report)): **$199 per state per year**, volume discounts to 40%, state filing fees invoiced separately. 4.3/5 from 212 Google reviews.

**Why the customer can't DIY:** fifty different secretaries of state, fifty deadline regimes, fifty fee schedules, and penalties or administrative dissolution for missing one. Constantly changing rules.

**What the human actually does:** imports registration data, **researches entity statuses**, determines exact filing fees, provides account management, and submits to each state.

**Software that exists:** registered-agent portals, and the compliance-automation layer — which is consolidating. **[Gusto acquired Mosey in July 2026](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/07/29/gusto-mosey-acquisition-multistate-compliance-guide)**, absorbing automated state and local registration, entity management and ongoing filings into a payroll platform.

**Scale of the underlying problem, from that article:** small businesses under 50 employees spend approximately **$14,700 per employee annually on regulatory compliance**, roughly 20% more per capita than larger firms, and about **15,000 new state and local laws pass yearly.**

**What remains manual:** entity status research, fee determination, and handling physical mail from state agencies.

**Recurrence:** annual per state, so a 10-state entity is 10 events a year.

**Liability:** low-to-moderate. A missed filing means penalties or loss of good standing — recoverable, and the obligation stays with the entity.

**Acquisition:** SEO on state-specific filing queries, accounting-firm partnerships, formation-service referrals.

**The trap — and it's serious:** the software layer is being bought by platforms. Gusto/Mosey is one data point; Middesk sells [payroll tax registration through payroll providers rather than direct](https://help.middesk.com/en/articles/8246273), which is the same pattern. A new entrant is building into a category where distribution is being captured by incumbents with the payroll relationship.

## D. Provider credentialing (dental and medical)

**What is sold:** getting a clinician enrolled and re-enrolled with insurance payers so the practice can bill them.

**Who pays:** dental or medical practice owner, or practice/office manager. Non-technical.

**How much:** mostly unpublished — which is itself a finding. [A 2026 buyer's guide covering ten credentialing companies](https://www.needletailai.com/blog/dental-rcm/dental-credentialing-software) reports that only one publishes a price: **Veritas Dental Resources at $5,995** for PPO fee negotiation bundled with credentialing. The rest quote privately.

**Why the customer can't DIY:** every payer has its own application, its own timeline, and its own failure modes; Medicaid rules vary state by state; and a denied application needs investigation and strategic resubmission rather than a resend.

**What the human actually does:** identifies which payer networks to join, manages applications and submissions, tracks approval timelines, resolves denials, and negotiates fee schedules.

**Software that exists:** CAQH/DataSpring — but note what it is. The guide describes it as *"a shared data platform that credentialing companies read from and write to—not a standalone solution practices use independently."* **The software in this category serves the service providers, not the end customer.**

**What remains manual, per that guide:** *"none of the ten in this guide publishes a specific denial-resolution process"*; recredentialing deadlines *"stack up across sites"* for multi-location groups; fee negotiation remains human. Its conclusion: *"Human oversight, not software efficiency, remains the differentiator in this market."*

**Recurrence:** recredentialing cycles (typically every 2–3 years per payer per provider), plus every new hire and new location.

**Liability flag:** healthcare-adjacent. It handles provider credentials rather than patient data, so it is administrative rather than clinical — but a failed credentialing means a practice bills for services it cannot bill for, which has payer-recoupment consequences. **Flagging this explicitly given your earlier constraint.**

**Acquisition:** dental and medical practice-management communities, DSO networks, practice consultants.

**The trap:** the highest-value part — PPO fee negotiation — is relationship-based consulting, not software. Strip it out and you're left with the lower-value form-tracking layer.

# Two things worth stating plainly

**The contingency-pricing problem applies to both of the strongest categories.** Chargeback representment and FBA reimbursement are the clearest cases in this research of humans still being paid in the presence of good software — and both are priced as a percentage of money recovered. That is not an accident of history. It is what buyers will accept for uncertain outcomes. Selling a subscription into either means asking merchants to pay whether or not you recover anything, against incumbents who only charge on success. Any plan there has to answer that objection first, and "our software is better" does not answer it.

**Category D contains the single most useful sentence found in this entire research programme:** *"Human oversight, not software efficiency, remains the differentiator in this market."* That is a third-party buyer's guide, not a vendor, concluding that software has not won. It is exactly the signal your new method was designed to find — and it comes attached to a healthcare-adjacent liability flag that you may well have already ruled out.

I found four, not ten. The method worked better than the previous ones — it produced verified prices, named vendors, and explicit statements about residual manual work in three of the four — but it did not produce abundance, and I'd rather hand you four real ones than ten padded.
